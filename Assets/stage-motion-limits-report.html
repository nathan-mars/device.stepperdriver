Move times and motion limits of the stage

Harp Stepper Driver, firmware 1.0, motor 0 on a 30 mm lead-screw stage (1.25 µm per pulse; stage design in Onshape). Bench test, 3 November 2026.

The question was whether the new trapezoidal motion engine moves the stage in the time the speed profile predicts, and how far speed and acceleration can be pushed on this stage before the motor loses steps. Each test homed the stage on its optical endstop, ran five out-and-back moves, timed each with the driver's own clock, and finished by finding the endstop again slowly to check that the position count still matched the stage. The motor current was at its maximum throughout.

Measured times follow the prediction

For every setting, the measured move time is within 2.4–3.1% of the time computed from the ideal trapezoid (constant acceleration from the start speed of 0.625 mm/s to the peak, cruise, and the mirror-image deceleration). The measured time is always slightly longer: by 4.4–4.5 ms for 15 mm moves and 6.4–7.1 ms for 25 mm moves. The offset is steady from run to run, so it can simply be added to a planned time. Repeats of the same move agree to about 0.05 ms, essentially the resolution of the timestamps.

Higher acceleration does shorten the move, but by less each time: going from 2.5 to 5.5 m/s² saves 27 ms on a 25 mm move (256 → 229 ms), and the next step would save only about 2 ms. On a 25 mm move, most of the time at the higher accelerations is already spent cruising at the top speed, as Figure 2 shows.

Limits of this stage and driver

Speed: 125 mm/s. The firmware's shortest pulse interval is 10 µs, which is 125 mm/s at 1.25 µm per pulse. The stage ran at that speed at every acceleration up to 5.5 m/s² without losing position, so the top speed is set by the firmware, not by the motor. Speeds between settings come in steps of whole microseconds per pulse: 12 µs gives 104.2 mm/s, 11 µs 113.6 mm/s, 10 µs 125 mm/s.

Acceleration: 5.5 m/s² at 125 mm/s. Raising acceleration in 0.5 m/s² steps, the stage ran cleanly up to 5.5 m/s². At 6.0 m/s² the motor lost synchronization 1.8 mm into the very first move, just after reaching full speed, and the stage stopped while the driver kept counting pulses. There was no gradual warning between the two settings. Since the current is already at its maximum, more torque is not available to raise this limit.

Suggested operating envelope

Peak speed up to 125 mm/s, acceleration up to about 5 m/s². That leaves one 0.5 m/s² step below the highest clean setting and two below the stall. Plan move times from the trapezoid and add about 7 ms (25 mm moves) or 4.5 ms (15 mm moves).

Two of the 17 test runs (at 3.0 and 3.5 m/s²) ended 14 and 35 µm off after five round trips; seven further runs at 3.5 m/s² were clean, and the cause was not found. Re-home periodically if micrometer-level position matters over many fast moves.

How it was measured

  • Stage: 30 mm lead-screw stage with an optical endstop at the home end; the CAD model is published in Onshape.
  • Script: Firmware/tests/hw/07_home_move_timed.py. Each move is timed from the driver's reply to the move command (the moment it starts the move) to its "motor stopped" event, both stamped by the driver's Harp clock, so USB delays do not enter.
  • The prediction uses the same settings as the driver: start and stop speed 0.625 mm/s (2000 µs per pulse), the commanded peak, and the commanded acceleration.
  • The endstop stays active during all moves, so a motor that loses steps is stopped by the sensor instead of running into the stage's hard stop. When the moves return all the way to home, the sensor stops them 15–30 pulses early: while decelerating, the carriage runs slightly ahead of the pulse count. A slow re-approach afterwards confirmed no position was lost in those cases. Moves ending 200 pulses (0.25 mm) short of home avoid this, and there the return moves take exactly as long as the outbound ones.
