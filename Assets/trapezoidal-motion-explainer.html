Trapezoidal speed control on the Harp Stepper Driver

Why it is possible on this microcontroller, what changed in firmware 1.0, and how fast several axes can move at once.

A stepper motor is moved by sending it a train of pulses; each pulse advances the shaft by one micro-step (1.25 µm on our lead-screw axes). Speed is simply how closely the pulses are spaced: 100 mm/s means a pulse every 12.5 µs, 1 mm/s means one every 1250 µs. A motor cannot jump from standstill to 100 mm/s; it must be accelerated, held at speed, and decelerated, so that speed plotted against time forms a trapezoid. Doing that in firmware means recomputing the pulse spacing as the speed changes, and the spacing is one divided by the speed. The driver's microcontroller (an 8-bit AVR with no floating-point hardware) needs about 20 to 28 µs to perform that division. The original firmware performed it after every pulse, which is arithmetically natural but physically impossible once pulses are closer together than the division takes. Hence the conclusion that a trapezoid could only be approximated, and only slowly: the "quick movement" mode that shipped had to spread one division across eight pulses and switch to a crude fallback at high speed, and ordinary moves used a simpler scheme that is not a trapezoid at all (the orange curve below).

The resolution is to separate two jobs that had been fused together. The pulses themselves are produced by a hardware timer that runs with no software involvement; firmware is only interrupted once per pulse to count it, which takes about 2 µs. The speed, on the other hand, does not need to be recomputed per pulse. Updating it one thousand times per second is more than enough: at 2.5 m/s² the speed changes by only 2.5 mm/s per millisecond, a step far smaller than the motor's own mechanical response can resolve. So the division (and one square root, used to decelerate so that the move lands on exactly the commanded pulse count) now runs once per millisecond in a low-priority "planner", costing about 50 µs of every 1000, independent of motor speed. Position is still exact because every pulse is counted in hardware-triggered interrupts; only the speed is piecewise constant over 1 ms windows. This is the same architecture used by essentially every CNC and 3D-printer controller on similar 8-bit processors, and it lets all four axes use true trapezoidal profiles, with travel limits and stop inputs honoured during the move, something the previous quick-movement mode could not do.

What runs when

How fast can several axes move at the same time?

Each pulse still costs the processor about 2.5 µs, so the total pulse rate of all moving axes is limited. The firmware applies a simple admission rule when a move is requested, and refuses the request (the register write returns an error, nothing moves) rather than running it badly. For axes with 1.25 µm per pulse the rule reads:

  • No single axis faster than 125 mm/s (10 µs between pulses).
  • The speeds of all axes moving at the same time add up to at most 150 mm/s. For example: one axis at 100 mm/s plus another at 50; two axes at 75; four axes at 37 each. Slow moves (a few mm/s) on all four axes together are always accepted.

Travel limits, stop inputs, the emergency input and position reporting work during every move, on every axis.
